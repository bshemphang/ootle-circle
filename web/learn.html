The idea behind the circle

Learn how a ROSCA works

A rotating savings circle is one of the oldest savings habits in the world. This page explains where it comes from, why it works, how it compares to a bank, and where it breaks.

An old idea, older than banks

Long before savings accounts, neighbours saved together. A group agrees on a fixed amount and a schedule. Each period everyone pays in, and one member takes the whole pot. The turn rotates until everyone has taken it once.

It is old

Rotating savings circles appear under many names across Africa, Asia, and Latin America, in communities where formal banking was absent or untrusted. The rules are simple enough to run on paper and strong enough to survive for generations.

It runs on trust

There is no collateral and no credit check. The guarantee is social: the members know each other, and the cost of defaulting is the relationship, not a penalty fee. That is why circles are usually small and the members are usually people who already trust each other.

It is not an investment

Nobody earns interest and nobody pays interest. Each member puts in the same total they take out. The value is timing: the member who takes the pot early gets a lump sum sooner, and the member who takes it last has saved steadily the whole way.

On-chain changes one thing

The organiser no longer holds the money. In a paper circle the organiser collects and hands out the pot, and the members must trust them with it. On-chain the contract holds the pot and follows the rotation, so no single person touches the funds.

Circle vs bank vs lending

A circle is not a replacement for a bank. It does one thing well and several things not at all. Here is the honest comparison.

The circle wins on discipline and on cost. It loses on flexibility: it cannot help a member who needs money outside their turn, and it cannot protect the group from a member who stops paying.

Questions

Is this real money?

No. The demo on this site is a simulation with play TARI. The contract itself accepts any fungible resource, and on-chain it would hold real TARI. Nothing on this site moves real funds.

What happens if someone stops paying?

The round stalls: no payout happens until everyone has paid. The creator can close the circle early, which refunds everyone who paid that round. Members who already took a payout keep it, because they paid their share in earlier rounds.

Does anyone take a fee?

No. The contract takes no fee. On-chain, the only cost is the network fee for each transaction, paid to Tari validators.

Is the contract audited?

No. It is unaudited. It is tested against the real Ootle engine, but that is not an audit. Do not put money you cannot afford to lose into any unaudited contract, including this one.

Why a fixed contribution?

A ROSCA works because everyone pays the same amount. A variable amount would let one member pay less and still take the full pot, which breaks the circle. The contract rejects any payment that is not exactly the contribution.

Who goes first?

The rotation order is the order members joined. Member 1 takes the pot in round 1, member 2 in round 2, and so on. There is no bidding and no lottery in this contract.

Can a circle have more than 8 members?

Not in this contract. The template caps a circle at 8 members, set at creation. A bigger circle means a longer wait for the last member and a bigger pot, and the cap keeps the circle short enough that the wait stays reasonable.
